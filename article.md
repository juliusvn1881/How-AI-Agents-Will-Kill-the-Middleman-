# How AI Agents Will Kill the Middleman

*Direct access and cheaper coordination could squeeze AI resellers while making new services worth buying.*

Say you pay an AI reseller because buying models from three different providers and wiring up their APIs is a pain. Then those providers make it easier to buy directly, and your agent can handle the integration. What would you still pay the reseller for?

That’s where I get stuck on the advice to “sell the picks and shovels.” Some resellers make money from how hard it is to buy and connect AI tools. The market for those tools can keep growing even as that part of the reseller’s job gets smaller.

## How resellers get squeezed

Start with access. An aggregator can charge for convenience when models or social media APIs are hard to buy directly. If an upstream provider opens up direct access, customers can go around the aggregator. That change is largely outside the reseller’s control.

Then there’s the work of making those services run together. If a buyer’s agent can compare prices, connect APIs, route requests, and keep integrations running, the buyer can bring that work in-house. Even customers who stick around may pay less once they can do more themselves.

Chips and compute still have to come from somewhere, and plenty of software does more than package another company’s API. The businesses I’m watching charge mostly for access and coordination.

## What the textile mills changed

In Britain’s putting-out system, merchants supplied materials to household spinners and weavers, arranged further processing, and sold the cloth. Coordinating production across those households was a real job.

Water-powered mills such as Arkwright’s brought machinery, power, and workers under one roof. Mills still needed supplies and distribution. But some of the coordination merchants used to do across households now happened inside the factory. The textile industry could grow without strengthening the merchants’ position.

![Before mechanization, merchants supplied materials and coordinated household spinning and weaving. Mills brought power, machinery, and workers together, moving some coordination into the factory. Procurement and distribution remained necessary.](diagrams/coordination-shift.png)

*The textile example shows how a change in production can move some coordination work inside the firm.*

Factories concentrated production. Agents can coordinate work that stays distributed. What makes the comparison useful is the shift in who does the coordinating work and who can charge for it.

## What becomes worth buying

A small merchant might want someone to track overseas competitors, adjust prices, and test copy across its catalog. Hiring a team to do that continuously could cost more than the work is worth, so it goes undone.

Suppose the merchant’s agent can buy competitor research, pricing analysis, and copy tests from other agents within a budget. If the results are worth the bill, the merchant has a reason to start paying for that work and to buy it again.

That’s the agent-to-agent opportunity I care about. A thousand API calls can go into a job the merchant already does. New demand shows up when the merchant starts buying a service it used to go without.

*For a merchant, the test is whether an ongoing service delivers enough value to justify paying for it.*

**AI usage could rise even as reseller margins fall.**

## Who gets the next sale?

An API reseller may have plenty of customers who wanted an easier way to buy an API. Those accounts tell you little about which customers would pay for something like ongoing competitor research, or whether they’d buy it through the same platform.

I pay attention to where developers already build and work together, including GitHub and startups making tools for agent builders. The test is whether builders keep using those platforms to get work done and whether buyers come back to pay for it.

At Penstairs, that’s where I start with GTM. I look for a job customers keep putting off because it costs too much, then ask what would make them pay for it regularly.
